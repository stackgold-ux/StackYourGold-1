## Part 1: Red Flags — Before You Touch the Metal

Most counterfeits reveal themselves before you reach for a testing kit.

### 🚩 Price Below Spot

Gold and silver trade on global spot markets. No legitimate dealer sells *below* spot. If someone offers a "1 oz Gold Eagle" for hundreds of dollars under spot, it's fake. No exceptions.

### 🚩 Suspicious Packaging

Counterfeiters have gotten good at replicating assay cards. Look for blurry logos, missing serial numbers, typos, or packaging that doesn't match the brand's current design.

### 🚩 Shaky Seller History

A brand-new account with a handful of reviews selling "estate find" gold bars is a giant red flag. Check how long the seller has been active and what their track record shows.

## Part 2: Five DIY Tests You Can Do at Home

### 1. The Magnet Test 🧲

**What you need:** A strong neodymium (rare-earth) magnet — not a fridge magnet.

**How it works:** Gold and silver are non-magnetic. If a magnet sticks hard, it's not precious metal. Interestingly, silver is slightly *diamagnetic* — a strong magnet will produce a subtle drag as it slides across a silver round.

**Limitations:** Catches iron and steel fakes, but not tungsten or copper cores. A first-pass filter, not a final answer.

**Cost:** ~$5–10.

---

### 2. The Ping Test 🔔

**What you need:** Your ears. Optionally, a "coin tester" app on your phone.

**How it works:** Balance a coin on your fingertip and tap it gently with another coin. Real gold and silver ring clearly and *sustain* the tone — like a bell. Counterfeits made from base metals produce a dull thud or a short, flat sound.

Silver has a distinctive high-pitched ring that lasts a second or two. Gold has a slightly lower, warmer tone. Both are unmistakable once you've heard them side-by-side with a fake.

**Limitations:** Works best on coins and rounds; bars don't ping as clearly. Won't catch tungsten cores, which ring similarly to gold.

---

### 3. The Specific Gravity Test ⚖️

**What you need:** A precision scale (0.01g accuracy), a cup of water, and thin thread. Total cost: ~$20.

**How it works:** Pure gold has a specific gravity of **19.32** — one of the densest elements on Earth. Pure silver is **10.49**. Nearly all counterfeit base metals are less dense.

**Steps:**
1. Weigh your item dry. Record the weight.
2. Fill a cup with water, place it on the scale, and tare to zero.
3. Suspend the item in the water on a thread — don't let it touch the bottom or sides.
4. Record the "wet weight" shown on the scale.
5. Divide dry weight by wet weight. That's the specific gravity.

**What the numbers mean:**

| Metal | Expected SG | Red Flag Below |
|-------|-------------|----------------|
| 24K Gold (.999+) | 19.32 | 18.5 |
| Silver (.999) | 10.49 | 10.0 |
| Copper | 8.96 | — |
| Tungsten | 19.25 | — |

If your "gold" bar has a specific gravity of 10, it's copper with gold plating. But note: **tungsten (19.25) is almost identical to gold (19.32)** — so this test alone won't catch a tungsten-filled bar. That's the most sophisticated counterfeit, and it needs the professional methods below.

---

### 4. The Ice Test ❄️

**What you need:** An ice cube.

**How it works:** Silver has the highest thermal conductivity of any metal. Place an ice cube on a silver round and it will start melting *immediately* — visibly faster than on steel, copper, or a countertop. Gold is also highly conductive, though slightly less dramatic than silver.

**Limitations:** Confirms the metal is highly conductive (good) but not *which* conductive metal. Pair with a magnet test for a solid two-step quick check.

**Cost:** Free.

---

### 5. The Acid Test (Use With Caution) ⚠️

**What you need:** A precious-metals acid testing kit (~$15–25).

**How it works:** Rub the item against the testing stone to leave a streak. Apply a drop of acid for the claimed purity. If the streak dissolves, the metal is below that purity; if it holds, it's genuine at that karat or higher.

**Warning:** This test is *destructive*. Don't use it on numismatic coins or anything you want to keep in mint condition. It's best for generic bars, scrap, or jewelry — and it still won't catch a thick plating over a tungsten core.

## Part 3: Professional Verification

When the stakes are high — or you're buying a significant amount — these methods provide definitive answers.

### X-Ray Fluorescence (XRF) 🔬

An XRF gun reads the elemental composition at the surface in seconds. Many coin and pawn shops have one and will test items for a small fee.

**Limitation:** XRF only reads the surface. A thickly-plated tungsten bar reads as pure gold. For thicker items, pair it with ultrasound.

### Ultrasound Testing 📡

Ultrasound sends sound waves through the *entire* thickness of a bar and reads how they travel. Different materials transmit sound at different speeds — so a tungsten core inside a "gold" bar shows up as an anomaly at the boundary. Professional dealers and refiners use this.

### Sigma Metalytics Verifier 💡

A handheld device that uses electromagnetic waves to read resistivity *below* the surface — deep enough to detect plating over a foreign core. It's the gold standard for retail-level verification and catches what XRF misses. (Units run roughly $800–1,500; many dealers have one.)
